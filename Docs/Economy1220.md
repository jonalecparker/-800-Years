# The Lord's Economy, c. 1220 — the research pass

*Compiled 2026-08-09 from four web-grounded research passes (income taxonomy;
demesne operations; tenants and mobility; expenses and prices). This is the
substrate for the economy design session (design doc open question #16) and
the phase-3 detail rulebook. Everything here is England/France c. 1180–1250,
reference year 1220. Numbers marked ⚑ are flagged: derived, interpolated, or
cited from memory rather than pinned to a verified source — good enough to
design against, check before shipping as lore.*

*Reading order: §1–2 are the shape of the answer to question #16. §3–8 are
the systems detail. §9 is the price book. §10 maps it all onto the game's
decided design and lists the decisions the design session has to make.*

---

## 1. Money, and the one sentence that calibrates everything

£1 = 20 shillings (s) = 240 pence (d). 1 mark = 13s 4d = ⅔£. France: £1
sterling ≈ 4 livres parisis ≈ 5 livres tournois.

**Calibration:** one penny = two-thirds of a laborer's day = a hen = half a
gallon of decent ale. One pound = 160 laborer-days = a draught horse = 10
days of a knight's campaign wages. £20/year = the whole living of a knight.
£1,000 = a small stone castle built over 7 years = King John's entire annual
castle budget across 95 castles.

**The inflation warning:** prices doubled or trebled between 1180 and 1220
(P.D.A. Harvey; Latimer compresses the shock into 1199–1206). Any price
recorded before ~1200 — including the famous castle totals — should be
roughly doubled to state it in 1220 money. The whole kingdom's circulating
coin in 1180 (£125,000) was about ten Château Gaillards; by 1278 it was
£674,000.

## 2. How a lord made money — the taxonomy and the split

The full stream list, with typical scale for a single decent manor:

| Stream | What it is | Typical scale |
|---|---|---|
| **Demesne produce sold** | Grain (wheat is THE cash crop), stock, wool, cheese | £10–30 grain in a normal year; wool £2–8 on a 100–300-sheep flock |
| **Assize rents** | Fixed cash rents, free + villein | ~6s per villein virgate where rent-based; free rents often token (pepper, a rose) |
| **Rents in kind** | Hens at Christmas, eggs at Easter, grain renders | Small but constant; whole manors could owe food-farms |
| **Labor services** | Week-work 2–3 days/virgate + harvest boons; sellable back as cash ("sale of works") | A virgate's full works valued ~16–20s/yr ⚑ |
| **Mill (banality)** | Suit of mill compels tenants; multure 1/13–1/24 of grain | £1–5/yr per mill, prime sites £10+; THE capital investment (§6) |
| **Court profits** | Amercements 2d–6d routine, licences, fees | ~5–10% of gross manorial income |
| **Servile incidents** | Tallage (~1–2s/virgate ⚑), entry fines (shillings in 1220), heriot (best beast), merchet (1s–10s), chevage, pannage | Steady trickle; entry fines are the one due that floats with the market |
| **Feudal incidents** | Relief (£5/fee, £100/barony — Magna Carta), wardship + marriage, aids, escheat | Zero most years; one good wardship = several years' income |
| **Market/fair/borough** | Tolls, stallage, burgage rents | Transformative where present: a planted borough yielded ~10–20× the same land's agricultural rent |
| **Wood/water/status assets** | Coppice (8s/acre/yr), fisheries (~13s 4d/yr), dovecote (2s–6s 8d/yr), parks, warrens | 2–5% oddments; warrens and parks are prestige, not income, in 1220 |

**The split is real, and it's a *policy outcome*, not a constant.** The two
best-documented profiles are near-opposites:

- **Honor of Gloucester, 1183–84** (old model, manors leased "at farm"):
  fixed renders **70.6%**, produce sales 18.0%, court profits 8.9%. Total
  £804/yr.
- **Cuxham, Oxon., c. 1240s** (small manor, direct management): rents +
  court profits only ~8% of ~£60 gross — the rest demesne produce, cereals
  5–8× livestock.

Rule-of-thumb for a directly-managed manor c. 1220 ⚑: demesne sales 40–60%,
rents + commuted works 25–40%, courts + incidents 8–15%, mill 5–10%,
oddments 2–5%. **Design gift: rentier vs. farmer isn't a historical footnote,
it's a slider the player sets** — and history just ran the experiment both
ways (§4).

## 3. The income ladder (where the player sits)

| Rank | Annual income |
|---|---|
| Day laborer | £1.5–3 (1–2d/day) |
| Villein virgater (30 acres) | subsistence + ~10–20s paid OUT in obligations |
| Parish vicar | £3 6s 8d statutory minimum (1222) |
| **Single knight's-fee manor** | **£10–20** (£20 = the distraint-of-knighthood threshold) |
| Gentry, 2–3 manors | £20–60 |
| **Minor baron** | **£100–200** ⚑ (verified examples: £138, £168, £181) |
| Major baron | £400–900 (Multon £537, Courtenay £489, Mortimer £485) |
| Earl | £500–3,000 |
| Bishop of Winchester (richest see) | ~£3,000–4,000 |
| Crown of England | ~£20,000–30,000 |
| Crown of France (1221) | ~£45,000–50,000 equivalent — the Capetians now out-earn the Angevins |

**The castle threshold falls out of this table**: a real stone castle
(£1,000+ to build, £10–50/yr to maintain and garrison) belongs to £200+
baronial incomes. A £15–20 knight kept a moated manor house or timber
ringwork. The player's arc from shabby-castle nobody to castle-age lord IS a
climb up this ladder, and the ladder is calibrated.

## 4. Why 1220 is the perfect reference year: the high-farming turn

Around exactly 1200, English lords stopped leasing their manors for fixed
rents and took the demesnes back into their own hands — because the
1180–1220 inflation had halved the real value of every fixed rent while a
lord selling grain caught the updraft. What direct management created:

- **Written administration was born.** Extents, surveys, the first
  manorial account rolls (Winchester pipe rolls begin 1208–09), auditors
  riding circuits, the reeve personally liable for arrears — accounts
  balanced to the farthing.
- **A professional staff**: steward over the estate (£2–10/yr + robes),
  bailiff per manor (~£2/yr + board), villein reeve (unpaid, rent-excused),
  famuli on cash + grain liveries.
- **Management literature**: Walter of Henley's *Husbandry*, Grosseteste's
  *Rules* — literal how-to-profit handbooks, full of fraud countermeasures
  (§7).
- Lords began to *measure* profit, compare manors, and push the dues that
  could move (entry fines) while custom froze the ones that couldn't.

A 1220 estate is an **early adopter** of this machinery — the player
inheriting a shabby castle and installing competent management is doing
exactly what the age was doing. France ran the opposite play: demesnes
stayed leased, and French lords inflation-proofed with *champart* (a
harvest-share rent, typically ⅛ of the crop) and heavier banalities (oven
and winepress monopolies England never had).

## 5. Running the demesne (the production game)

- **Land**: typical demesne ~100–200 sown acres (a fifth to a third of the
  manor; the rest is tenant land). Two- or three-field rotation; fallow
  grazed ("each fallow acre supports two sheep at the least" — Grosseteste).
- **Crops have jobs**: wheat = the cash crop (50–90% sold); barley = ale;
  oats = horses and pottage (mostly consumed); rye/maslin = servants' bread;
  legumes = pottage and fodder.
- **Yields are brutal**: seed 2 bu/acre (wheat); actual returns ~3.8–4× seed
  for wheat, ~2.4× for oats ⚑. **Walter of Henley: 3× seed is breakeven** —
  "you will gain nothing unless corn sells well." Bad harvest ~1 year in 8;
  dearth years spike grain 2–3×, which is a *windfall* for a lord with a
  full grange and a disaster for everyone else.
- **Sheep are the growth asset**: **Grosseteste's constant — 1,000 sheep in
  good pasture = 50 marks (£33)/yr** (wool + lambs + dairy + skins). A sack
  of wool ≈ 200–260 fleeces, £2.5–5 ⚑; Flemish merchants buy the clip,
  Italian houses will pay cash years in advance (effectively loans). Breed
  improvement was already practiced (Downton bought Lincolnshire rams,
  1208).
- **Oxen vs horses** (Walter's worked numbers): a working horse costs 12s
  5d/yr, an ox 3s 1d/yr — and a worn-out ox is dinner while a dead horse is
  a hide.
- **Pigs pay only where there's pannage woodland**; grain-fed swine "lose
  twice as much as you gain."
- **Labor is three streams**: famuli (5–10 permanent farm servants — the
  skilled year-round core, paid small cash + big grain liveries, e.g. a
  ploughman at 2s + 6½ quarters of barley); villein week-work (thrown mostly
  at harvest, much of it commuted to cash — though lords were clawing
  services back in exactly this period); hired day labor (threshing all
  winter at piece rates, harvest hands).
- **The year has a shape the player will SEE** (feeds the seasons/walking
  baseline): Michaelmas (29 Sep) is the hinge — account rendered, reeve
  elected, seed changed, stock culled. Martinmas slaughter. Winter
  threshing in the barn. Spring ploughing and lambing. June shearing — the
  wool clip bagged for the merchant. August–September harvest: the whole
  manor mobilized, boon-works fed bread and ale at the lord's cost. 44
  working weeks a year; a plough does ~⅞ acre a day.

## 6. Mills (the model capital investment)

Windmills are *brand new* in 1220 (post mill invented c. 1180) — this is the
period's tech boom. Build cost ~£8–12 (windmill) to £10–20+ (watermill) ⚑;
revenue £1–5/yr typical, prime sites £10+; **payback ~2–5 years**, guaranteed
by suit of mill (tenants MUST grind at the lord's mill; multure 1/13–1/24 of
the grain). Costs: millstones £1–2 each, constant maintenance, and a miller
everyone assumes is skimming. Abbot Samson of Bury swore he wouldn't eat
bread until a rival's private windmill came down — the monopoly was worth
defending. This is the cleanest template for the game's build-asset →
income-stream loop, and the same shape covers the French oven/press
banalities, fisheries, dovecotes, and markets.

## 7. Stewards, accounts, and fraud (the treatises are a design spec)

The chain: **steward** (whole estate, holds courts, visits each manor 2–3×/yr,
must "know the law of the realm") → **bailiff** (per manor, the hired
professional, up at dawn) → **reeve** (a villein *elected by the tenants* as
"the best husbandman," personally liable, one-year term "if he be not proved
most profitable") → **hayward** (field sergeant). Real pay: steward's fee
£1–2 per manor served, bailiff ~£2/yr, at Forncett the manor grossed £128
and paid £23 total expenses.

**Competence was measured by the audit**: the Michaelmas account charges the
reeve with everything he *ought* to have received; the shortfall is his
personal debt. Auditors compared quarters harvested against quarters sown
and could disallow a bad yield unless he could swear cause (murrain, storm).
Grosseteste's verification tricks: sworn inquest of 12 free men per manor;
throw every eighth sheaf into a measure at the grange door as a yield
sample; have the stacks valued at Michaelmas and hold the officers to the
estimate.

**The fraud catalogue** (all from the treatises — ready-made steward-tier
content): heaped vs razed bushels ("four heaped up will make five");
grange-keeper and barn-keeper colluding; threshers smuggling corn in boots
and pockets; reeves trading with the lord's money; selling a beast and
reporting it dead (hence mandatory carcass inspection before flaying);
dairymaids skimming cream so "the cheese shall be less." Walter's deterrent
is the player's verb: **"Look into your affairs often... for those who serve
you will thereby avoid the more to do wrong."** And the personnel doctrine:
choose bailiffs "not for kindred or liking"; independently "inquire by his
own men" about the steward's doings.

## 8. The people (what they owe, what binds you, how they leave)

- **The village**: ~40–60% villeins, 20–30% free tenants, ~30% smallholders/
  cottars (the wage-labor pool — under ~15 acres can't feed a family without
  selling labor). Standard holding: the virgate, ~30 acres.
- **A virgater's bundle** (Alwalton benchmark): 3s 1d cash rent + 3 days/week
  work + kind-rents (bushel of wheat, hens, eggs) + merchet + tallage +
  heriot (best beast) + suit of mill. Total burden ≈ a quarter to a third of
  gross output ⚑ — before the Church's tithe takes 10% off the top.
- **Custom genuinely bound the lord** (the key modern finding — Kanzaka):
  villein rents, protected by custom of the manor, were usually LOWER than
  market freehold rents. The manor court's facts were found by tenant
  jurors. Tenants of former Crown manors could sue any lord who raised
  services above Domesday levels (villages pooled money to search Domesday).
  Resistance before 1250 was legal-collective, not violent: withdrawal of
  services (the medieval rent strike), go-slows, pooled lawyer funds. The
  humiliations that actually rankled: merchet, tallage-at-will, the best
  beast taken at a widow's worst moment.
- **The manorial bargain** (what they demanded back): security of tenure and
  heritability, protection, predictable justice, famine relief, commons
  access (pasture, wood, peat), a working mill.
- **Exit was real but lived at the margin**: a runaway who reached a
  chartered borough, the royal demesne, or another county was usually gone
  (writ of naifty in theory, expensive pursuit in practice; "town air makes
  free" after a year and a day — narrower in practice than in legend). But
  c. 1220 population pressure meant a queue for every vacant holding — the
  settled core wasn't the flight risk. **The margin was**: cottars, younger
  sons, skilled workers — and lords openly competed for them. The Charter of
  Lorris (light fixed dues, no tallage, year-and-a-day amnesty) was copied
  by 80+ French towns; the Law of Beaumont by ~500 — and incumbent lords
  swore it to their own tenants to stop the bleeding. England's version:
  planted boroughs (burgage: 12d/yr, personal freedom) and assarts on free
  terms.
- **What attracted settlers** (from the charters themselves): personal
  freedom (no merchet/tallage/week-work), low fixed rent, a standard plot,
  capped and predictable justice, toll exemptions, infrastructure (market,
  mill, church, bridge, castle protection), no questions about your past.
  Housing itself was rarely the advertised good — **terms and plots were;
  the settler built the house**. But lords DID invest: rent-free years and
  structural timber granted on condition the tenant build better ⚑ (the
  documented form of the housing loop) — and better buildings enabled
  higher-value work (brewing, dairying, textiles).
- **A peasant house** cost ~40s and lasted a generation; stone footings
  (the 13th-c. upgrade) stretched that toward centuries. A good house was a
  visible capital asset — legible on a walk.

## 10. Game hooks — the research mapped onto the decided design

**Question #16, answered in structure.** The historical answer to "demesne
produce vs. rents and fees" is: *both, and the mix is the lord's strategic
choice.* Leasing/rents = low effort, low risk, inflation-eroded, steward-
light. Direct management = high effort, high yield, harvest-risk, and it
*requires* the steward/account machinery. That maps beautifully onto the
game: the rentier estate runs itself badly (and quietly shrinks); the
managed estate is the game. Income streams then slot into three families —
**produce** (demesne + flock, sold at market, price-volatile), **dues**
(rents, court, incidents — custom-capped, stable, politically loaded), and
**franchises** (mill, oven, market, borough — built assets with capex and
payback). Franchises are the bridge between the builder and the economy.

**Stewards.** The audit IS the mechanic: a steward's competence tier =
how much leaks between what the estate *ought* to yield and what reaches
you; the period's own fraud catalogue (§7) is the flavor text; Walter's
"look into your affairs often" makes the player's first-person presence —
walking the estate, showing up at the Michaelmas account — mechanically
meaningful, which is exactly the two-altitudes pillar. The historical
steward also *held the courts* → dues income depends on him too.

**Housing loop.** Historically the lord supplied *terms and timber*, not
finished houses: rent holidays + building timber conditional on the tenant
building better, and better buildings enabled higher-value tenant work.
The recruitment side ran on charters: freedom, fixed rent, plot,
predictable justice, infrastructure. So "spend → better workers" is
historically two levers: **build infrastructure** (mill, market, church,
housing aid) and **grant terms** (lower dues, freedoms) — the second costs
income rather than cash, which is a nice tradeoff.

**Workers leave — with a design tension to resolve.** History says: the
settled core didn't flee (land hunger meant a queue for every holding);
custom, courts, and collective resistance disciplined the lord there.
Exit power lived at the margin — cottars, younger sons, skilled workers,
famuli — and *there* lords genuinely competed (Lorris, Beaumont, planted
boroughs). Options for the game: (a) fidelity — exit pressure scales with
how little the worker holds, and the landed core pushes back through
custom/court friction instead of leaving; (b) the decided simplification —
everyone can leave, custom mechanics folded into "unhappy workers leave."
The design session should choose deliberately.

**Wall types (the build prerequisite).** The research gives the ladder:
palisade/timber ≈ £10–30 in materials + owed labor; a brattice £2–5; stone
curtain ~£10–20/perch ⚑; small stone castle ~£1,000 over 7 seasons; keep
8–10 seasons at ~£150/season; Gaillard proves money compresses time nearly
linearly (a decade's budget in 2 years). Build season is April–November
(mortar can't cure in frost) — construction pauses in winter, which feeds
the visible-seasons baseline. Maintenance is real but small (£2–20/yr).
Masons 3–4d/day, laborers 1½d/day, a modest project = a few dozen men.

**Special rooms (#14) candidates the period actually had**: the grange +
granary (the yield sample thrown at its door), the account/counting room
(the Michaelmas audit), the chapel + chaplain (5 marks/yr), the hall
(hospitality = renown), the dovecote/fishpond/park (status monopolies),
the wool store (the clip awaiting the Flemish buyer), the mill.

**Renown (#11) hooks from the period**: famine relief (dues relieved in
dearth was a real expectation), hospitality and feasts (Christmas robes,
the great household), generosity vs. the humiliating dues (merchet,
heriot enforcement) — the same levers that historically made a "good
lord."

**Threats and pressure**: the era's real financial predators were the
crown (arbitrary fines pre-Magna Carta, wardship auctions) and debt (43%/yr,
manors as security — Aaron of Lincoln died holding £15,000 of knightly
debt, and the crown collected). A money-trouble spiral (borrow → mortgage
a manor → lose it) is period-true failure texture short of siege.

**Phase 1 fit.** "Day One" = walk the shabby estate and make first
decisions through people. The research hands Phase 1 its calendar beat:
arrive at Michaelmas — the account is rendered, the reeve is elected, the
seed is changed, the stock culled. The inherited estate's books, read to
you by whatever steward your father left, ARE the tutorial.

## 11. Standing flags

- Proportion tables and the 1220 price interpolations are design baselines,
  not citations — the underlying sources range 1208–1348 and the great
  inflation sits mid-window. Anything pre-1200 doubled; anything post-1250
  shaded down.
- Weakest numbers: wool per sack (£2.5–5), mill build costs (£8–12),
  1220-decade grain prices, per-perch curtain walling (derived from 1280s
  Caernarfon), merchet/tallage typical amounts, household share of income
  (half to two-thirds).
- The management treatises (Seneschaucy, Walter of Henley) describe 1260s–80s
  practice; Grosseteste (c. 1240) is closest to the window. Institutions all
  existed by 1208; the polish came later — a 1220 estate is an early adopter.
- Interpretive fight worth knowing: exploitation-centred (Hilton) vs.
  custom-as-contract (Kanzaka) readings of villeinage. Both are usable: the
  rules were custom-bound, the humiliations were real.
